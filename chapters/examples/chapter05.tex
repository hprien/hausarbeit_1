\chapter{AI driven Penetration Testing}
\label{ch:chapter05}
Penetration Tests can either be performed by a software vendor it self or they can be delegated to a third party specialist. Costs that could be caused by data breaches and exploited vulnerabilities are significantly higher than costs of Penetration Tests. \cite{Gaafar_2024} However Penetration Tests are time consuming and labour intensive thus expensive dough. \cite{Confido_2022}

Tools like BurpSuite, ZAP and SQLMap already implement automated penetration testing techniques through signature-driven \ac{DAST} scanning approaches that systematically map web applications, fuzz parameters with curated payloads, and detect injection vulnerabilities like XSS, SQLi, and command injection. They can be compared to \ac{DAST} scanning approaches that employ structured workflows including spidering/crawling for endpoint discovery, active scanning with payload databases, passive analysis of HTTP responses, and specialized exploitation modules for database fingerprinting and evasion techniques. However, these approaches are fundamentally constrained by their pattern-matching limitations that prevent the contextual reasoning, multi-step exploitation chains, and novel attack creativity required to identify complex business logic vulnerabilities and zero-day exploits. \cite{Osman_2025}

Earlier approaches to automate the process of penetration testing like ESA's PenBox leveraged Reinforcement Learning. PenBox has the capability to execute given actions like port scanning with Namp, SSH security testing with Hydra and exploitation of known vulnerabilities with Metasploit. Training landscapes were built to train PenBox to use given actions beneficially. The training and test environment in the case of PenBox comprises 8 hosts in one network. \cite{Confido_2022} Corporate networks span way larger which lets approaches like PenBox reach its limits. Training tools like PenBox requires high fidelity training environments that often concentrate on exploiting known vulnerabilities through Metasploit. The inability to adopt to dynamically changing environments makes Reinforcement Learning approaches unable to identify complex business logic vulnerabilities and zero-day exploits. \cite{Schwartz_2019}

Leveraging \ac{LLM}s for penetration testing shows better results and scalability in comparison to Reinforcement Learning. \ac{CTF} Challenges from HackTheBox and VulnHub were used to evaluate the performance of GPT-4 without any technical additions. GPT-4 was prompted to advice the user to execute a specific task like running a \ac{CLI} command which's output the user prompts back to the \ac{LLM}. Such a technically minimalistic setup proves that \ac{LLM}s are capable of performing penetration tests as GPT-4 is able to solve 4 easy and 1 medium challenge. This benchmark was executed on 13 targets from HackTheBox and VulnHub covering OWASP Top 10 and categories easy, medium and hard. The domain knowledge was entirely limited to the \ac{LLM}s inherit domain knowledge. \cite{Deng_2024}

The process of penetration testing can be split up into multiple tasks. Which allows to deligate different tasks to different \ac{LLM} agents that are specialized in certain fields mainly by their system prompts. The architecture of PentestGPT introduces three agents to delegate these tasks to. One agent responsible for information condensing, one for reasoning and one for concrete task planing. This agentic approach further improves the \ac{CTF} challenge completion rate. Without the agentic approach GPT-4 was able to solve 4 easy and 1 medium challenge with the agentic setup it is able to solve 6 easy and 2 medium challenge out of the same set of challenge. The increase in \ac{CTF} challenge completion rate points to the significance of the \ac{LLM} orchestration. \cite{Deng_2024}

One limiting factor towards higher \ac{CTF} challenge completion is the lack of up-to-date domain specific knowledge such as known vulnerabilities. The knowledge of an \ac{LLM} is entangled within thousands of parameters. Retraining \ac{LLM}s with up-to-date information is computationally very expensive. \ac{RAG} introduced by Lewis et al. \cite{lewis_2020} is a common approach to provide \ac{LLM}s with knowledge it has not inertly build in. The additional information are stored as embeddings in a vector database. The \ac{LLM} is then provided with retrieved information from the \ac{RAG}'s vector database. \cite{lewis_2020}

PentestAgent improves the architecture of PentestGPT by introduces domain specific knowledge on penetration testing and known vulnerabilities. It equips the agentic setup with a \ac{RAG}. Through this external knowledge base multiple vulnerability databases are available to PentestAgent. Through several adjustments regarding the agentic setup and the additionally introduces domain specific knowledge PentestAgent outperforms PentestGPT by far when it comes to solving \ac{CTF} challenges. 11 \ac{CTF} challenges were randomly selected from HackTheBox (nine easy, one medium and one hard). PentestGPT was able to solve 5 of these challenges (all easy) while PentestAgent was able to solve 9 of these challenge (7 easy, one medium and one hard). \cite{Shen_2025}

The success of PentestGPT and PentestAgent are not limited to small networks or even single hosts. \ac{AI} Agents and thus \ac{LLM}s are also capable of performing Penetration Tests in real world and real size networks. ARTEMIS is a agentic approach like PentestGPT and PentestAgent. However it was optimized and tested in a real world university network. Two versions of ARTEMIS were benchmarked against experienced human penetration testers. The first ARTEMIS version running entirely on GPT-5 came up with 11 findings out of which 55\% were valid findings. The second ARTEMIS version running on multiple different \ac{LLM}s was able to come up with 11 findings as well. However, 82\% of them were valid also their severity was much higher. Both versions are able to compete with human penetration testers. The architects of ARTEMIS mention that the agents scans systems in a broad mana instead of moving lateral like experience human penetration testers do. By moving lateral human penetration testers tend to make findings with higher severities. \cite{Lin_2025}

Real world corporate applications are much more complicated and unknown than test environments like DVWA, OWASP Juice Shop and OWASP WebGoat. They have dynamic pages with JavaScript logic and proprietary, sometimes niche technology stacks. Automated testing tools need to traverse the detailed architecture of the system under test. XBOW is a \ac{LLM} powered automated penetration testing tool that publicly claims to be able to work with corporate applications and all its challenges. XBOW can operate with and without access to the source code of the system under test. \cite{xbow2026} Not many information about the detailed system structure are made public however XBOW's high position on the Hackerone leaderboard proves that it is clearly capable of making valid findings in corporate infrastructure. \cite{hackerone_leaderboard} \cite{xbow_hype}

Corporate security experts who used XBOW on proprietary web applications shared their experiences for this term paper. To evaluate XBOW's performance, the corporate security experts used a deliberately vulnerable web application based on a proprietary technology stack as a benchmark system. The experts found that XBOW was unable to identify any vulnerabilities in this system. XBOW was provided with source code and network access to the application. According to the experts, XBOW is capable of finding vulnerabilities in other applications, but they report that the tool tends to produce a high rate of false positives. The experts also note that valid findings made by XBOW are generally low-hanging fruits from the perspective of experienced penetration testers.

Software vendors like Google implement \ac{LLM} powered penetration testing tools into software security processes. Google's open-source oss-fuzz for example is a \ac{LLM} powered fuzzing tool that actively found many security flaws in open source software. \cite{Happe_2025}

\section{Security Challenges}
\label{sec:chapter05:sec_challenges}
\ac{LLM}s are trained based on a set of data. These data do not encompass the entirety of available information. As not even the entirety of information necessarily represents truth, \ac{LLM}s have inherit bias from their training data. This can lead to unwanted treatment of actual vulnerabilities. A biased \ac{LLM} can potentially overlook vulnerabilities. \cite{Hilario_2024}

In the context of Penetration Testing \ac{LLM}s are often times equipped with tools like web search. Prompts can be injected into the \ac{LLM}'s context through the access of third party webpages. In the combination with the \ac{LLM} holding confidential data, the risk of leaking these information to a third party webpage rises.
